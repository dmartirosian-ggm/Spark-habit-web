A chore chart for a toddler isn’t about getting the housework done. A two year old “helping” to unload the dishwasher takes longer than doing it yourself. What a chore chart does at this age is teach your child that they’re part of the family team, that jobs have a beginning and an end, and that doing them feels good.

This guide covers which chores fit each age from 2 to 6, how to set up a chore chart that a child who can’t read can still follow, how to handle rewards, and what to do when the novelty wears off in week two. There’s a [free printable chore chart](/printables/chore-chart/) at the end.

## Why chore charts work for young kids

Kids between 2 and 6 want to do what grown-ups do. They want to stir, sweep, carry, and press buttons. A chore chart takes that natural interest and gives it a shape:

- **It makes expectations visible.** A toddler can’t hold a list in their head, but they can look at a picture of a toothbrush and a picture of a bed and know what comes next.
- **It turns “because I said so” into “because the chart says so.”** The chart becomes the neutral referee, which takes some of the power struggle out of it.
- **It shows progress.** A row of stickers or stars is proof your child can point to. At this age, seeing is believing.
- **It builds a habit before it’s a chore.** Kids who put toys away at 3 because it’s just what happens after playtime are much easier to work with at 8.

The trick is that a chore chart for a young child looks very different from one for a ten year old. It has fewer jobs, more pictures, and a much shorter wait for the payoff.

## Chores by age

Every child is different, so treat these lists as a menu, not a test. Pick two or three jobs your child can mostly do on their own, plus one that’s a little bit of a stretch. All of these assume a grown-up is nearby, and many will need you to do them together at first.

### Chores for 2 year olds

At two, a chore is any small, one-step task with an obvious finish. Show them, do it with them, and celebrate wildly.

- Put toys in a bin (one bin, not sorted)
- Put dirty clothes in the hamper
- Throw away a diaper or wipe in the trash
- Put books back on a low shelf
- Carry their own cup or plate to the counter
- Wipe up a small spill with a cloth
- Help feed a pet (you measure, they pour)
- Bring you their shoes when it’s time to go out

### Chores for 3 year olds

Three year olds can handle two-step tasks and start to remember a short routine with reminders. This is a great age to introduce a picture chore chart.

- Make the bed by pulling up the covers (lumpy is fine)
- Sort toys into two or three labeled bins
- Help set the table with napkins and spoons
- Put clean socks into a drawer
- Water plants with a small watering can
- Wipe the table after a meal
- Help put groceries away in low cupboards
- Match socks from the laundry basket

### Chores for 4 year olds

At four, kids can follow a routine with less prompting and take real pride in doing a job “all by myself.”

- Make the bed (covers and pillow)
- Get dressed independently and put pajamas away
- Set the table with plates, cups, and silverware
- Clear their own dishes to the sink or dishwasher
- Help unload the dishwasher (plastic and silverware first)
- Feed a pet on their own with a measuring scoop
- Dust low surfaces with a sock on their hand
- Help sort laundry by color

### Chores for 5 year olds and kindergarteners

Five and six year olds can manage a short daily list and start helping with whole-family jobs. They’re also ready for a chore chart that runs for the whole week.

- Pack and unpack their backpack
- Make their bed every morning
- Put away their own clean laundry
- Help prepare simple food: wash fruit, spread butter, pour cereal
- Empty small trash cans into the big one
- Sweep with a small broom and dustpan
- Water plants and fill the pet’s water bowl
- Tidy their bedroom with a simple checklist

### Quick reference table

| Age | How many chores | What a “chore” looks like | Reward timing |

## How to make a chore chart that works

### 1. Start with three chores

The most common mistake is a chart with ten rows. Pick three things, and make at least two of them easy wins. You can always add more once the first three are automatic.

### 2. Use pictures, not words

Kids under five mostly can’t read, so every chore needs a picture. Photos of your child’s own things work even better than clip art: their toothbrush, their toy bin, their bed. If you’re making your own chart, ask your child to help draw the pictures. They’ll feel ownership over it.

### 3. Tie chores to a time of day

“Tidy up toys” floats around all day and never happens. “Tidy up toys before dinner” has a trigger. Group chores into morning, after nap or school, and before bed. This is how routines work, and it’s why our [toddler routines guide](/toddler-routines/) uses the same approach.

### 4. Show, then do it together, then watch

For each new chore, show your child how to do it, then do it together for a few days, then let them do it while you watch. Only then expect them to do it on their own. Skipping straight to “go clean your room” is how chore charts end in tears.

### 5. Let your child mark the chart

The sticker, the stamp, the check, or the tap on the app is the best part. Let your child do it themselves, right after the chore is done, not at the end of the day.

### 6. Praise the effort, not the result

“You put every block in the bin!” lands better than “Good job.” Describe what they did. A bed that’s lumpy but made by a three year old is a made bed.

### 7. Review once a week

On Sunday, look at the chart together. Count the stars. Talk about which job was the hardest. Swap out a chore that’s become too easy or keeps getting skipped.

## Should you reward chores?

This is the question parents argue about most. Here’s a practical middle ground that works for most families with young kids:

- **Some jobs are just family jobs.** Putting away your own toys and clothes in the hamper are things everyone in the family does. These can go on the chart for the visual reminder and the satisfaction of a sticker, without a bigger reward attached.
- **Stars toward a goal work well for new habits.** When you’re introducing a new routine, a star for each job that adds up to something fun (a trip to the park, choosing dinner, an extra bedtime story) gives kids a reason to push through the learning curve.
- **Keep rewards small and experience-based.** You don’t need toys. Our guide to [reward charts](/reward-charts/) has a long list of rewards that aren’t candy or stuff.
- **Money can wait.** Allowance tied to chores can work for school-age kids, but most kids under 6 don’t understand money well enough for it to mean much.

Whatever you choose, never take stars away for bad behavior. The chart should only ever go up. If a chore didn’t happen, that space just stays empty.

## Common chore chart problems (and fixes)

**“My child loved it for a week, then lost interest.”**
That’s normal. Refresh it: let your child choose a new reward, add a new “grown-up” chore they’ve been asking about, or move the chart somewhere new. Kids also lose interest when the reward is too far away. Shorten the goal.

**“They only do it for the sticker.”**
At 2 to 4, that’s fine. The sticker is the bridge to the habit. Once a chore has been automatic for a few weeks, take it off the chart and replace it with something new. That’s the point where it’s just “what we do.”

**“My child refuses.”**
Check the chore is really doable. Then make it a game: race the timer, sing a clean-up song, or do it as a team. Offer a choice between two chores so your child has some control.

**“We have two kids and it turns into a competition.”**
Give each child their own chart with chores that fit their age. Compare each child to themselves (“You did more than last week!”), not to their sibling.

**“We keep forgetting to fill it in.”**
This is the biggest reason paper charts fail. Put the chart where the chores happen, or use an app on your phone so it’s always with you.

## Chore chart FAQ

### What age should you start a chore chart?

Most kids are ready for a simple picture chore chart around age 3. Two year olds can do chores too, but they usually do better with you doing the job together and celebrating right away than with a chart.

### How many chores should a 3 year old have?

Two or three is plenty. Pick one they can do alone, one they can do with help, and maybe one that’s a little stretch.

### Should chores be the same every day?

For kids under 5, yes. Repetition is what turns a chore into a habit. Older kids can handle a chart where some chores are only on certain days.

### What if my child doesn’t do the chore right?

Let it go. A toddler-made bed is a made bed. You can gently show them again tomorrow, but don’t redo it in front of them. That teaches them their effort doesn’t count.
