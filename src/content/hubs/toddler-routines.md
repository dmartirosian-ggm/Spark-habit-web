Toddlers and preschoolers thrive on predictability. When they know what comes next (breakfast, then teeth, then shoes), there’s less to argue about and less to be anxious about. A good routine isn’t a rigid schedule. It’s the same handful of steps, in the same order, at roughly the same time each day.

This guide covers why routines matter for kids 2 to 6, how much sleep they need, a simple bedtime routine, a morning routine that gets you out the door, sample daily schedules by age, and how to use a picture routine chart. There are free printable [bedtime routine](/printables/bedtime-routine-chart/) and [morning routine](/printables/morning-routine-chart/) charts too.

## Why routines help young kids

- **Fewer power struggles.** When brushing teeth always comes after pajamas, it’s just the next step, not a new demand.
- **Smoother transitions.** Young kids find switching activities hard. A routine tells them a change is coming.
- **More independence.** Kids who know the steps can start doing them on their own, especially with a picture chart.
- **Better sleep.** A consistent bedtime routine is one of the most reliable ways to help young children fall asleep more easily.
- **Less mental load for you.** You stop making the same decisions every day.

## How much sleep does your child need?

Sleep recommendations from the American Academy of Sleep Medicine, endorsed by the American Academy of Pediatrics, are:

| Age | Total sleep in 24 hours (including naps) |
| --- | --- |
| 1–2 years | 11 to 14 hours |
| 3–5 years | 10 to 13 hours |
| 6–12 years | 9 to 12 hours |

Work backward from your child’s wake-up time. If your 3 year old wakes at 7:00 and naps for an hour, a bedtime between 7:00 and 8:00 p.m. usually fits. Most toddlers drop their nap somewhere between ages 3 and 5, and an earlier bedtime often helps during the transition.

## A simple toddler bedtime routine

A bedtime routine for a toddler or preschooler usually takes 20 to 30 minutes. The specific steps matter less than doing them in the same order every night. Here’s a routine that works for many families:

1. **Wind-down signal (5 minutes before).** “Five more minutes, then it’s bath time.” Dim the lights and switch off screens.
2. **Bath or wash (10 minutes).** A warm bath isn’t needed every night, but a quick wash still marks the shift.
3. **Pajamas on.** Let your child choose between two pairs.
4. **Brush teeth.** Let them brush first, then you finish.
5. **Potty.** Last trip before bed.
6. **Books (10 minutes).** Agree on the number of books up front: “Two books tonight.”
7. **Lights out.** A short song, a cuddle, and the same goodnight phrase every night.

**A few tips that make bedtime easier:**

- **Screens off an hour before bed** when you can. Bright screens and exciting shows make it harder to settle.
- **Offer small choices** (which pajamas, which book) so your child feels in control of something.
- **Keep it the same at grandma’s.** Bring the routine with you when you travel, even a shortened version.
- **Handle stalling with structure.** Build in one “last thing” (a sip of water, one more hug) so they don’t have to invent five.

## A toddler morning routine

Mornings fall apart when every step is negotiated. A short, visual morning routine helps a lot, especially if you need to get to daycare or school.

1. **Wake up and potty**
2. **Get dressed.** Lay clothes out the night before.
3. **Eat breakfast**
4. **Brush teeth**
5. **Shoes and coat on**
6. **Grab backpack** (for preschoolers and up)

Put the steps on a [morning routine chart](/printables/morning-routine-chart/) by the front door or bedroom, and let your child mark each step as it’s done. If mornings are especially rushed, move anything you can (choosing clothes, packing the bag) to the night before.

## How to use a routine chart

Kids who can’t read can still follow a routine if every step has a picture. A routine chart turns “What do I do now?” into a quick glance.

- **Keep it short.** Five or six steps per routine is plenty.
- **Use real pictures.** Photos of your child doing each step, or of their own things, are easiest to recognize.
- **Put it where it happens.** Bedtime chart in the bedroom or bathroom, morning chart by the door.
- **Let your child mark it.** A sticker, a magnet, or a check makes finishing satisfying.
- **Add a small reward for full routines** when you’re starting out. Our [reward charts guide](/reward-charts/) explains how to keep rewards simple.

If chores are part of your day, our [chore charts by age](/chore-charts/) guide shows how to add a job or two to the afternoon routine.

## When routines don’t go to plan

**Your child stalls at bedtime.** Start the routine earlier so you’re not rushing, and keep the steps identical each night. A visual chart helps your child see there’s an end.

**The routine falls apart on weekends.** Try to keep wake-up and bedtime within an hour of weekdays. The order of steps matters more than the exact time.

**A new sibling, move, or illness throws everything off.** That’s normal. Go back to the basic version of the routine and add steps back over a week or two.

**Two caregivers do it differently.** Agree on the steps together and use the same chart, or share a routine app so everyone follows the same plan.

## Toddler routine FAQ

### What time should a toddler go to bed?

Most toddlers and preschoolers go to bed between 6:30 and 8:00 p.m. Work backward from their wake-up time and the total sleep they need, which is 11 to 14 hours for 1 to 2 year olds and 10 to 13 hours for 3 to 5 year olds, including naps.

### How long should a toddler bedtime routine be?

Around 20 to 30 minutes is typical. Long enough to wind down, but not so long that it becomes a negotiation.

### When should I start a routine chart?

Most kids can use a simple picture routine chart from about age 2 and a half to 3. Younger toddlers benefit from routines too, without the chart.

### Should weekend routines be the same?

Keep the order of steps the same, and try to keep bedtime and wake-up within about an hour of weekdays.
