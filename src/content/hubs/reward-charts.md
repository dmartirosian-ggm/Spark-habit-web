A reward chart is one of the simplest tools in parenting: your child does the thing, they get a sticker, and when the chart is full they earn a reward. When it works, it feels like magic. When it doesn’t, it’s a sheet of paper with three stickers on it, stuck to the fridge since March.

The difference is almost always in the setup. This guide covers how reward charts and sticker charts work, how to set one up for a toddler or preschooler, reward ideas that aren’t candy or toys, the mistakes that quietly sink most charts, and when to stop using one. There are free printable [sticker charts](/printables/sticker-chart/) and [reward charts](/printables/reward-chart/) to get you started.

## Reward chart vs. sticker chart: what’s the difference?

People use the terms interchangeably, but there’s a useful distinction:

- **A sticker chart** tracks one behavior, and the sticker itself is the reward. For a two year old, putting the sticker on the chart is the whole thrill.
- **A reward chart** collects stickers or stars toward a bigger goal, like a trip to the park after ten stars. It works better from about age 3, when kids start to understand “later.”

Most families start with a simple sticker chart for a toddler and move to a reward chart with a goal as their child gets older.

## Do reward charts work?

Used well, yes. Reward charts are a form of positive reinforcement: noticing and rewarding a behavior you want to see more of. The same idea, often called a token system, is a core part of well-studied parent training programs for young children.

They work best when:

- **The behavior is specific.** “Use the potty” or “stay in bed after lights out” works. “Be good” doesn’t, because a three year old has no idea what it means.
- **The sticker comes right away.** Young children connect a reward to whatever happened just before it. A sticker at dinner for something done at breakfast barely registers.
- **The goal is reachable.** For a toddler, that means days, not weeks.
- **It comes with praise.** The sticker is a signal. Your attention and specific praise (“You stayed in bed all night!”) are what really matter.

A common worry is that rewards will make kids only do things for a prize. For brand-new skills and routines in young children, that’s rarely a problem in practice, as long as you phase the chart out once the habit is solid. Think of it as scaffolding: you take it down when the building can stand.

## How to set up a reward chart for a toddler

### 1. Pick one behavior to start

The best first chart focuses on one thing. Choose something your child can already do sometimes, so they get early wins. Common first charts:

- Using the potty (see our [potty training chart guide](/potty-training-chart/))
- Staying in bed at bedtime
- Brushing teeth without a fight
- Getting dressed in the morning
- Tidying up toys

### 2. Say it in words your child understands

Describe the behavior as what to do, not what to stop. “Feet stay on the floor at dinner” beats “No climbing on the table.” Practice it together so they know what earning a sticker looks like.

### 3. Size the goal to your child’s age

| Age | Spaces on the chart | Time to reward |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | 3 to 5 | A day or two |
| 3 | 5 to 10 | A few days |
| 4 | 10 to 15 | About a week |
| 5–6 | 15 to 20 | One to two weeks |

If your child is losing interest, the goal is probably too far away. Make the next chart shorter.

### 4. Choose the reward together

Kids work harder for a reward they picked. Give two or three options you’re happy with and let them choose. Drawing a picture of the reward on the chart is a powerful reminder.

### 5. Let your child add the sticker

Make it a moment. Let them choose the sticker, put it on, and count how many they have. Then tell them exactly what they did to earn it.

### 6. Celebrate the finish

When the chart is full, deliver the reward quickly and make a big deal of it. Then start a new chart, maybe with a new behavior or a slightly bigger goal.

## Reward ideas for toddlers that aren’t candy or toys

The best rewards for young kids are experiences and time with you. They cost little, they don’t clutter the house, and kids genuinely love them.

**Time together**

- An extra bedtime story
- A special breakfast date with one parent
- Building a blanket fort together
- Baking cookies or muffins together
- A dance party in the living room

**Choices and privileges**

- Choosing what’s for dinner
- Picking the movie for family movie night
- Choosing the bedtime song or book
- Sitting in the “special seat” at dinner
- Choosing the weekend outing

**Outings**

- A trip to the playground or splash pad
- Visiting the library to pick new books
- A trip to the zoo, aquarium, or farm
- Feeding ducks at the pond
- A walk to get a treat at a favorite café

**Small things they can use**

- Bath crayons or bubbles
- A new set of stickers for the next chart
- Sidewalk chalk
- A coloring page printed just for them

## Reward chart mistakes to avoid

**Taking stickers away.** A chart that can go down becomes a punishment chart, and kids quickly decide it’s not worth trying. If your child doesn’t earn a sticker, that space simply stays empty.

**Charting too many behaviors at once.** Five behaviors means five things to remember, five ways to fail, and a confused toddler. One or two at a time.

**Vague behaviors.** “Being nice to your sister” is too fuzzy. “Using gentle hands with your sister” is better, and “Sharing a toy when she asks” is better still.

**Moving the goalposts.** If you agreed on ten stars, the reward comes at ten stars, even if the last few days were rough.

**Forgetting about it.** Charts fail most often because grown-ups forget to update them. Consistency matters more than the chart design. If you’re not home much, or several adults share the routine, a reward chart app keeps everyone on the same page.

**Making it the only tool.** A chart doesn’t replace connection, routine, or simply helping your child do the thing. It’s a boost, not a fix.

## When to stop using a reward chart

Plan the exit from the start. Good signs it’s time to move on:

- Your child does the behavior most days without reminders
- They’ve stopped asking about the sticker
- The chart has been running for a few weeks with steady success

Phase it out gently. Move to a bigger goal with fewer stickers, then switch the chart to a new behavior. Keep the praise going even after the stickers stop. If the old behavior slips, it’s fine to bring the chart back for a week.

## Reward chart FAQ

### What age is a reward chart for?

Simple sticker charts work from around age 2. Reward charts with a bigger goal work best from about age 3 to 8, while kids are still motivated by visible progress.

### How many stickers before a reward?

For a 2 or 3 year old, 3 to 10. For a 4 to 6 year old, 10 to 20. Shorter is better if you’re not sure.

### Should siblings share a chart?

Give each child their own chart with goals that fit their age. Shared charts tend to turn into competitions.

### What if my child cries when they don’t get a sticker?

Stay calm and kind. Say something like, “Not this time. You can try again tomorrow.” Avoid giving the sticker anyway, or the chart loses its meaning.
