A potty training chart is one of the most popular tools parents reach for, and for good reason. Potty training is a long string of small wins: sitting on the potty, telling you they need to go, actually going, washing hands. A chart makes each of those wins visible to a child who is working really hard at something new.

This guide covers how to tell if your child is ready, what to reward at each stage, how to set up a potty training sticker chart, what to do about accidents, and when to stop using the chart. You’ll also find a [free printable potty training chart](/printables/potty-training-chart/).

> This guide is general information for parents, not medical advice. If you have concerns about your child’s toileting, talk to your pediatrician.

## Is your child ready for potty training?

There’s no perfect age. Many children show signs of readiness somewhere between 18 months and 3 years, and girls are often ready a little earlier than boys. Readiness matters much more than age. Starting before your child is ready usually means a longer, more frustrating process.

Signs your child may be ready:

- They stay dry for two hours or more during the day, or wake up dry from naps
- They notice when they’re going, by hiding, pausing, or telling you
- They show interest in the toilet or in wearing underwear
- They can pull their pants up and down, or nearly
- They can follow simple two-step directions (“Get your shoes and bring them here”)
- They’re bothered by a wet or dirty diaper

If your child resists strongly, or there’s a big change at home (a new baby, a move, starting daycare), it’s usually fine to wait a few weeks and try again.

## How a potty training chart helps

Potty training asks a lot of a toddler. They have to notice a body signal, stop playing, get to the bathroom, manage clothes, and sit long enough. A chart helps because:

- **It rewards trying, not just succeeding.** Early on, simply sitting on the potty deserves a sticker.
- **It makes progress concrete.** “Look how many stickers you have!” is more motivating than “You’re doing great.”
- **It gives your child some control.** Choosing and placing the sticker is something they get to own.
- **It gives everyone the same plan.** When a partner, grandparent, or daycare knows what earns a sticker, your child gets a consistent message.

## What to reward, stage by stage

The biggest mistake with potty charts is only rewarding success. In the beginning, success is rare and your child will lose heart. Reward the next small step instead, and move the target as they improve.

| Stage | What earns a sticker | Roughly when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Getting comfortable | Sitting on the potty, even with clothes on | First few days |
| 2. Trying | Sitting with a bare bottom for a minute or two | First week |
| 3. Success | Peeing or pooping in the potty | Once trying is easy |
| 4. Telling you | Saying they need to go before it happens | As they start noticing |
| 5. Staying dry | A dry morning, a dry nap, a dry outing | Later on |

Many families also add a sticker for washing hands afterwards. It builds a lifelong habit while the routine is new.

Poop is often harder than pee, and some kids are nervous about it. It’s fine to give a bigger reward, like two stickers, for poop in the potty.

## How to set up a potty training sticker chart

1. **Print or make a simple chart.** Our [potty training sticker chart](/printables/potty-training-chart/) has 30 spaces. For a young toddler, a shorter [10-sticker chart](/printables/sticker-chart/) may be better.
2. **Hang it in the bathroom** at your child’s eye level, so the sticker goes on right away.
3. **Explain it simply.** “When you sit on the potty, you get a sticker. When the chart is full, we’ll go to the playground!”
4. **Let your child pick the reward** from two or three options. Our [reward charts guide](/reward-charts/) has lots of ideas that aren’t candy.
5. **Give stickers immediately** and pair them with specific praise. “You felt it and came to the potty. That’s amazing!”
6. **Keep potty breaks regular.** Offer the potty when your child wakes up, after meals, before and after naps, before leaving the house, and before bed.
7. **Count the stickers together** at the end of the day.

## What to do about accidents

Accidents are part of potty training, not a sign it’s failing. Most kids have them for weeks or months, especially when they’re busy playing, tired, or in a new place.

- **Stay calm and matter-of-fact.** “Oops, you had an accident. Pee goes in the potty. Let’s get clean clothes.”
- **Never punish, shame, or take stickers away.** Fear of getting it wrong can lead to holding it in, which makes things harder.
- **Involve them in cleanup** in a low-key way, like carrying wet clothes to the laundry basket. It’s about responsibility, not punishment.
- **Look for patterns.** If accidents happen at the same time each day, add a potty break just before.

## Daytime vs. nighttime training

Staying dry at night is a separate milestone from daytime training. It depends on a child’s body making less urine at night and waking to the signal, which they can’t learn from a chart. Many kids are dry during the day long before they’re dry at night, and it’s common for children to still wet the bed at age 5 or older.

Most pediatricians suggest keeping nighttime pull-ups until your child is waking up dry most mornings on their own. Don’t put nighttime dryness on a reward chart. Your child can’t control it, and missing out on stickers for something they can’t help is discouraging.

## Regression: when a trained child starts having accidents

It’s common for a child who has been using the potty to start having accidents again. Common triggers include a new sibling, starting school or daycare, a move, illness, or constipation. Go back to basics: more regular potty breaks, more praise, and bring the chart back for a while.

Check in with your pediatrician if your child has pain when going, blood in their urine or stool, constipation, sudden accidents after being reliably trained for a long time, or if you have concerns about progress as they approach age 4.

## When to stop using the potty chart

Most families stop the chart once their child is using the potty reliably during the day and telling you when they need to go. Phase it out rather than stopping suddenly:

1. Move to a bigger reward that takes more stickers.
2. Switch from a sticker for every success to a sticker for a fully dry day.
3. Finish the last chart with a big celebration, like a “big kid” outing.

## Potty training chart FAQ

### What should a potty training chart reward?

Start by rewarding sitting on the potty. Once that’s easy, reward actually going in the potty, then telling you they need to go, then staying dry. Always reward the next step, not the end goal.

### How many stickers should a potty chart have?

For a 2 year old, 5 to 10 spaces with a small reward at the end works well. For 3 year olds and up, 20 to 30 spaces is fine.

### What age should you start potty training?

There’s no single right age. Many children are ready between 18 months and 3 years. Look for readiness signs rather than a date on the calendar.

### Do potty training charts work?

For most kids, yes, as long as the chart rewards small steps, stickers come right away, and accidents are never punished. The chart works best combined with regular potty breaks and lots of praise.
