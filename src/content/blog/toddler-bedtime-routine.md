If bedtime in your house is a nightly negotiation (one more book, one more drink, one more hug), you’re in good company. The good news: a short, predictable bedtime routine is one of the most effective things you can do for a toddler’s sleep. Not because any one step is magic, but because doing the same steps in the same order every night tells your child’s brain that sleep is coming.

This is the 30-minute plan we recommend for kids ages 2 to 3. It fits into the bigger picture of [toddler routines](/toddler-routines/) for the whole day, and it works with or without a bath.

## Before you start: pick a bedtime

Most toddlers need 11 to 14 hours of sleep in 24 hours, including naps. Count back from when your child usually wakes up. If they wake at 7:00 a.m. and nap for two hours, lights out around 7:00 to 7:30 p.m. is a good target. Start the routine 30 minutes before that.

Keep bedtime within about an hour on weekends, too. A bedtime that moves around a lot makes the routine harder to stick to.

## The 30-minute toddler bedtime routine

### 0:00 — The wind-down signal

Give a heads-up: “Five more minutes of play, then it’s bath time.” Use a timer if your child likes to argue with you. Arguing with a timer is less fun. Dim the lights in the main room and turn off the TV and tablets.

Screens are worth calling out. Bright, exciting screens right before bed make it harder for kids to settle, so aim to have them off about an hour before lights out when you can.

### 0:05 — Bath or wash-up (10 minutes)

A warm bath is a classic, calming signal, but it doesn’t have to be every night. On non-bath nights, a quick wash of face and hands at the sink keeps the step in place. Keep bath toys calm and the water play short. This isn’t the time for a big splash session.

### 0:15 — Pajamas on

Offer a choice of two pairs. Toddlers want control, and small choices give them some without derailing the plan. If getting dressed is a battle, try making it a race: “Can you get your pajamas on before I count to 20?”

### 0:18 — Brush teeth

Let your child brush first, then you take a turn to make sure every tooth gets cleaned. Use a rice-grain smear of fluoride toothpaste for kids under 3, and a pea-sized amount from age 3. A two-minute song helps time it.

### 0:21 — Potty

Even if your child is still in diapers or pull-ups at night, a last sit on the potty builds the habit. For kids who are potty training, this step matters even more.

### 0:23 — Books in bed (5 to 7 minutes)

Agree on the number before you start: “Two books tonight.” Let your child choose them, then read in bed or in the same chair every night. Lower your voice as you go.

### 0:30 — Lights out

End with the same short ritual every night: a song, a cuddle, and the same goodnight phrase. Then leave the room. Consistency here is what makes the routine work.

## Make the routine visible with a chart

Toddlers can’t read, but they can follow pictures. A [bedtime routine chart](/printables/bedtime-routine-chart/) with a picture for each step (bath, pajamas, teeth, potty, books, lights out) shows your child where they are and that the end is coming.

Let your child mark each step as it’s done. For many kids, a sticker or star for finishing the whole routine is enough motivation for the first few weeks. If you want to add a reward for a week of good bedtimes, our guide to [sticker and reward charts](/reward-charts/) explains how to size the goal for a toddler.

## Handling the classic bedtime problems

### Stalling

“I need water.” “I need to tell you something.” “One more hug.” Build the most common requests into the routine: a sip of water after teeth, one extra hug at lights out. When the requests are already covered, there’s less to invent.

### Curtain calls

If your child keeps coming out of their room, walk them back calmly with as little talking as possible: “It’s sleep time. I love you. Goodnight.” Do it the same way every time. It can take several nights, but boring and consistent wins in the end.

### “I’m scared”

Take fears seriously, but keep it brief. A night-light, a favorite stuffed animal, and checking under the bed together as part of the routine can help. Avoid long conversations about monsters right at lights out.

### Early waking

If your toddler wakes up too early, check that bedtime isn’t too late (overtired kids often wake earlier), the room is dark enough, and naps aren’t too long. A toddler clock that changes color when it’s OK to get up can help kids 2.5 and older.

### Two caregivers, two routines

If one parent does bath-books-bed and the other does books-bath-bed, your toddler will notice. Agree on the order together, and use the same chart.

## What if the routine stops working?

Routines often wobble during big changes: a new sibling, a move, starting daycare, illness, or dropping a nap. Go back to the simplest version of the routine for a week or two, keep lights out at the same time, and add steps back once things settle.

If your child regularly takes more than 30 to 45 minutes to fall asleep, snores loudly, or wakes many times a night, mention it to your pediatrician.
